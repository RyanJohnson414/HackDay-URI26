Fish in a school adjust their speed and direction in response to nearby neighbors. Each fish uses local sensory information to keep an appropriate distance, align with nearby movement, and turn away from a threat. The response of one fish changes the signals available to the fish around it, creating a feedback loop across the group. Repeated local adjustments allow a school to move as a coordinated group, split around an obstacle, and change course without a leader planning the motion of every fish.

Researchers describe these interactions using models of connected agents: each agent receives information from nearby agents and updates its own behavior. The group-level pattern comes from many local interactions rather than a central instruction. This is useful to compare with other networked systems, while remembering that fish use sensory and motor behavior rather than explicit numerical training rules.
