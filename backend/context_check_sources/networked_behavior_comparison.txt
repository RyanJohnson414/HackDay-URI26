Fish schools and artificial neural networks can both be described as systems of connected units whose local inputs influence a larger pattern. A fish changes its movement using sensory information about nearby neighbors; a neural-network unit changes its activation using weighted signals from connected units. In both cases, interactions among units shape the system's behavior or output.

The comparison is structural rather than literal. Fish respond through biological sensing and movement, while an artificial network performs mathematical operations and learns by adjusting weights from prediction error. Comparing the systems can help researchers ask how local information, connectivity, and feedback contribute to coordinated behavior, without claiming that fish perform backpropagation or that artificial units reproduce animal brains.
