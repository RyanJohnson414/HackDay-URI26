An artificial neural network represents information through connected units. During a forward pass, each unit combines signals from earlier units using connection weights and applies an activation function; the resulting activity moves through layers to produce an output. Training compares that output with a target and adjusts the weights to reduce prediction error. During backpropagation, the error signal is propagated through the network so each weight is updated according to its contribution to the output. Repeated examples gradually change the network's response.

This provides a useful comparison with other systems made of interacting units: local inputs and connection strengths influence activity, and many updates create a system-level result. The analogy to fish behavior or biological learning has limits. Artificial weights and backpropagated gradients are mathematical components, not direct copies of synapses, animal decisions, or known mechanisms of memory consolidation in the brain.
